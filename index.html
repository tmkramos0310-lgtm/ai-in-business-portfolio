About This Portfolio

A Quarter-Long Exploration of Real AI

This portfolio documents seven hands-on labs completed in MIS 432: AI in Business at Western Washington University, each one built around how a real company — from a drug-repurposing startup to an autonomous vehicle giant — designed, deployed, and governed an AI system. In each lab, I wrote working Python code, built simplified versions of actual production systems, and published the results publicly on GitHub Pages.

Across these projects, I built a knowledge graph for drug discovery, simulated Netflix-scale A/B tests, implemented collaborative filtering, forecast ride demand, classified images with a neural network, modeled dynamic pricing for a two-sided marketplace, and prototyped an agentic AI pipeline for healthcare. What surprised me most wasn't how powerful these systems are — it's how much invisible infrastructure, governance, and human judgment sits behind every model.

The biggest gap I found this quarter was between the hype and the implementation: AI doesn't replace human decisions so much as it reshapes which decisions humans still have to make, and when. These labs gave me a concrete, technical vocabulary for that reality — one I can bring to any data-facing role in business.

Big-Picture Insights

Key Takeaways

What I Actually Learned This Quarter

01

AI is only as good as its data pipeline. Every lab started with data cleaning, structuring, or generation — and every production failure case we studied traced back to data quality, not model architecture. The unsexy infrastructure layer is where the real leverage is.

02

Business value comes from workflow integration, not model accuracy. A more accurate recommendation engine that nobody acts on is worth less than a slightly less accurate one embedded directly into the moment of decision. The gap between a prediction and a decision is where most AI value gets lost.

03

Every AI system needs a human-in-the-loop governance plan before it ships. Whether it's a drug-repurposing score reviewed by a clinician or an insurance appeal flagged for a human advocate, the questions of who reviews what, when, and under what conditions aren't afterthoughts — they define the system's trustworthiness.

04

The real skill is knowing what question to ask. Across all seven labs, the moments of genuine learning came not from running the model but from asking: whose interests does this algorithm serve? What does it fail to see? What would have to be true for this prediction to be wrong? That critical lens is more portable than any specific technique.
